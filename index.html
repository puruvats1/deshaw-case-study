Interaction Design Intern · Summer 2026 · 8 weeks

Rethinking data visualization for better company-wide adoption

D.E. Shaw is a major international hedge fund, where decisions run on data. I spent the summer of 2026 there as an interaction design intern, working on one of the firm's key in-house tools used across business units to turn data into charts, dashboards and shared reports.

Over four weekly sprints, I took its redesign from research to a working MVP prototype, along with a multi-state component library and the interaction logic behind it.

The tool itself was remarkably capable. It could handle almost any chart a team needed.

And yet, people used it reluctantly, and often ended up in Excel or a general-purpose AI assistant instead.

01Why would people work around a tool built for exactly this?

Picture an analyst who needs a simple chart: how trading volume has changed by region over the past year.

The tool can build it. Over the years, it has grown from a simple chart-and-table tool into something that can handle almost any chart. But between what the analyst wants and the chart itself sat panels, settings and a long list of fields. For many people, it wasn't worth the effort, and adoption stayed low.

AI had arrived as one more layer on top. It could make the chart, but it sat apart from the rest of the work.

My brief was not to polish it, but to rethink it from the ground up, AI-first. The team wanted more control, without the complexity. Context on what is happening, without the labour of scavenging for the right data.

02What if AI wasn't another layer?

Imagine the same chart, but the AI is already where the analyst is working: in the data, in the chart, in each setting, with a conversation there when it's needed.

It does the hunting.

The analyst makes the decisions.

That was the lens for this redesign: AI integrated at a molecular level, with a human in the loop.

Designing for AI is mostly designing for control.

Each what-if below is a question I set out to answer, and each answer became a working prototype.

04Where does a chart actually begin?

Before anyone starts building one.

Back to our analyst. Before any chart, they need the right fields, in a dataset they may never have opened.

  • Is there a region field?
  • Which one is volume?
  • Is there a date to track change over?

The answers sat in one long list, each field labelled in technical terms.

Now suppose the dataset could introduce itself, showing what it covers in plain terms before a single field.

Suppose fields came in groups people already reason with.

Think of a coffee order:

dimensionsThe size and the flavour are dimensions, things you sort by.
measureThe price is a measure, something you add up.
timeAnd when you ordered it is time.

For our analyst, region is a dimension and volume is a measure. And suppose a search for "geography" still found region.

The hunt would mostly disappear.

05What happens between an idea and a chart?

The analyst knows what they want to see. Getting there usually meant one of two things:

an AI-made chart that was hard to adjust,

or an empty builder waiting to be filled in.

But what if they could describe the chart in their own words, and get back something they could read: a plain description of the chart where every part can be changed? Fixing one word would replace starting over.

What if the AI knew which part of the chart they meant?

They wouldn't need to explain it.

And what if it showed what it was doing while it worked?

A wrong answer would become a small correction, not a dead end.
